Operators of public spaces: it's up to you to improve indoor air quality!

Discover which concrete steps you can take to improve indoor air quality in your location. This way, you reduce the impact on your visitors’ health. Use practical guides and free apps. Learn when you’ll be required to take action to improve air quality in your business or organisation.

Why is indoor air important for your health?

The air in your location plays a major role in the comfort and well-being of your visitors. If the air contains too many pollutants, this can be harmful to their health. That’s why it’s important to improve air quality and create a healthy and pleasant environment.

Want to know more? Read all about the impact of indoor air on health here.

Are you required to take action?

All indoor spaces that are enclosed by walls or doors, and have a ceiling or floor, fall under the law of 6 November 2022 on improving indoor air quality in enclosed spaces accessible to the public, unless they are limited to the family sphere or purely the professional sphere.

This means you must follow the rules if you manage a location where the public is welcome, such as hospitality businesses, cultural institutions, sports facilities, care institutions, schools and training centres, shops and retail outlets. On the contrary, private dwellings and meeting rooms reserved exclusively for employees, for example, are not.

A voluntary phase until 2027

Since 1 October 2024, a voluntary phase has been in place. It gives you the opportunity to get familiar with the requirements of the law on indoor air quality.

From 2027, a Royal Decree will define which types of public spaces are subject to these obligations. This list will gradually expand, so that by the end of 2037, all publicly accessible indoor spaces will fall under the law.

By starting voluntarily now, you’ll be well prepared for future obligations. At the same time, you’ll already offer your visitors a healthier and more pleasant environment.

Note: some places fall under the Codex on Well-being at Work and must already comply with indoor air quality standards, separate from this legislation.

What must you do?

From today, you can voluntarily apply the first measures of the law:

  1. Measure the CO2 concentration to check whether there is sufficient ventilation for the number of people present and the activity taking place. A practical guide helps you choose a suitable device and explains how to install, use and maintain it correctly.

  2. Identify pollution sources and carry out a risk analysis. Identify the factors that affect air quality in your space. If needed, create an action plan to show which alternatives you’ll use and how you will manage the risks. Consult the step-by-step guide and use the free online application Risk analysis & action plan to fill in these documents.

  3. Answer questions from your visitors about the CO2 concentration or airflows in your space.

To improve air quality in your space:

  • reduce pollution sources
  • ventilate the space
  • purify the air

Follow the detailed recommendations on the page about improving indoor air quality.

Belgium, leading the way in indoor air quality

Indoor air quality is a major public health concern, and Belgium is taking the lead thanks to the law of 6 November 2022. With this ambitious legislation, we are setting an example for broader efforts at European and international level. The goal is clear: ensure healthy indoor air today and for future generations, and protect vulnerable people who may be more affected by repeated exposure to invisible but harmful substances.

Thanks to this law, we are making the invisible visible: clear information about indoor air quality in public spaces allows you to make conscious choices based on health and personal concerns. This approach raises awareness for you as an operator of public spaces, and for your visitors, while also helping prevent future health crises. This law is part of a broader strategy to protect both our health and our activities in the long term.

To ensure better indoor air quality, reliable and effective equipment is needed. That’s why we are imposing health requirements on manufacturers and importers of CO2 meters and air purification systems sold in Belgium.

Frequently asked questions

  • What are my obligations as the operator of a publicly accessible space?

    At this time, as the operator or owner of an enclosed publicly accessible space, you are not yet subject to any obligations under the law of 6 November 2022. Since 1 October 2024, you may apply the law on a voluntary basis. See the section “What must you do?” for more details.

    From 1 January 2027, these measures will gradually become mandatory. We recommend that you already take part in the voluntary phase so you are well prepared for the legal deadlines.

  • Why do I need to measure CO₂ levels?

    A high CO2 level in the air indicates insufficient ventilation in relation to the number of people present and the activity in the room. This doesn’t necessarily mean that the air contains viruses, but if someone is sick, micro-organisms, bacteria or viruses may be present in the air they exhale. A high CO2 level is a sign that you need to ventilate or air out the space.

    Poor ventilation also leads to a build-up of other pollutants in the air. Measuring CO2 levels tells you when it’s time to refresh the air or turn on an air purifier.

    Note: a CO2 meter only measures the CO2 level in the room. The device itself does not provide ventilation or fresh air.

  • How do I know if the CO₂ values I measure are reliable and useful?

    To get reliable measurements, you must place, calibrate and maintain your CO2 meter correctly. Follow the instructions in the practical guide.

    At first, it’s useful to measure in different spots in the room. This shows you where air circulation is more difficult. A second guide helps you calculate an average CO2 value for your space.

    Ideally, the CO2 concentration stays below 900 ppm (maximum 1,200 ppm). These values correspond to ventilation rates used as references in the law of 6 November 2022. The required ventilation differs depending on the activity and how much energy people use. The guide explains which values apply to your situation.

  • What are the reference levels for indoor air quality under the law?

    The law of 6 November 2022 defines two indicative reference levels:

    • Level A: CO2 concentration below 900 ppm or a minimum airflow of 40 m³ per hour per person, with at least 25 m³ of outside air.
    • Level B: CO2 concentration below 1,200 ppm or a minimum airflow of 25 m³ per hour per person.

    These levels are not mandatory but serve as targets to assess and improve indoor air quality.

    They match the standards in the Codex on Well-being at Work. These standards are mandatory for rooms covered by the Codex.

  • What is a risk analysis and action plan for indoor air quality?

    A risk analysis identifies the factors that affect indoor air quality, such as ventilation, the number of people and their activity, the materials used, etc.

    An action plan builds on this and describes the measures you’ll take to improve air quality, with short-, medium- and long-term deadlines.

    Use the free application Risk analysis & action plan to complete these documents easily online. A practical guide will assist you step by step, including how to use the application.

  • Why is ventilation important in enclosed public spaces?

    Ventilation brings in fresh air and removes polluted air. This dilutes pollutants such as micro-organisms, chemical substances and fine particles. It reduces health risks for people in the room.

  • What is the difference between ventilation and airing out?
    • Ventilation is the controlled supply of outdoor air, using natural systems (like ventilation grills) or mechanical systems.
    • Airing out means opening windows or doors temporarily to refresh the air.

  • Are air purifiers needed in addition to ventilation?

    Yes. Air purifiers complement ventilation by removing specific pollutants that ventilation alone may not address, or that are brought in from outdoors.

    However, they do not replace ventilation. They do not bring in fresh air and cannot remove gases like CO2.

    It’s best to use both systems together for optimal indoor air quality.

    Want to choose an air purifier suited to your needs? Read the practical guide.
